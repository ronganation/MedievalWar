# Overview

The Realm Governance Framework governs how realms administer territory, delegate authority, manage expansion, and maintain control.

The framework exists to provide a middle ground between:

Total War

↓

Direct ownership of everything

and

Crusader Kings

↓

Extensive vassal micromanagement

The goal is to create meaningful governance decisions without overwhelming complexity.

---

# Core Philosophy

Power becomes harder to manage as realms expand.

Small realms may be governed directly.

Large realms require delegation.

Governance is not simply about owning territory.

Governance is about managing people.

The framework attempts to answer:

- Who controls territory?
- Who governs territory?
- Who receives authority?
- How much power should be delegated?
- How does expansion affect administration?

---

# Design Principles

Avoid:

- Full CK3 vassal simulation
- Hundreds of contracts
- Excessive bureaucracy
- Settlement-by-settlement politics

Avoid:

- Total War direct ownership of everything forever

Focus on:

- Important people
- Important regions
- Meaningful authority decisions
- Strategic governance

---

# Realm Structure

Every realm consists of:

- Royal Domain
- Governed Regions
- Frontier Regions
- Church Territories
- Dynastic Holdings

The size and composition vary by faction.

---

# Royal Domain

The Royal Domain consists of territories directly controlled by the ruler.

Examples:

England

- Winchester
- Key Royal Holdings

Normandy

- Rouen
- Ducal Holdings

---

## Benefits

- Maximum control
- Higher revenue
- Faster decision making

---

## Drawbacks

- Administrative burden
- Limited capacity
- Scaling difficulties

---

# Domain Capacity

Every ruler possesses a practical governance limit.

Domain Capacity represents:

How much territory can be effectively administered directly.

Factors:

- Administration
- Government Type
- Technology
- Laws
- Character Traits

---

## Exceeding Capacity

Small Excess:

- Reduced efficiency

Moderate Excess:

- Stability decline

Severe Excess:

- Corruption
- Loyalty loss
- Administrative strain

This replaces artificial hard limits.

---

# Governed Regions

Governed Regions are territories administered by trusted figures.

Governors retain authority while serving the realm.

Examples:

- Earl
- Count
- Duke
- Governor
- Satrap
- Regional Lord

depending on faction flavor.

---

# Governor Characters

Governors may be:

- Dynasty Members
- Nobles
- Courtiers
- Military Leaders
- Religious Figures

Governors are characters first.

Administrators second.

---

# Governor Evaluation

Key Factors:

- Loyalty
- Competence
- Prestige
- Influence
- Dynasty Membership

Selecting governors becomes a strategic decision.

---

# Frontier Commands

Frontier Commands represent territories requiring military oversight.

Examples:

- Welsh Marches
- Scottish Borders
- Frontier Counties
- Steppe Border Regions

Frontier Commanders emphasize:

- Defense
- Security
- Military readiness

over economic administration.

---

# Marcher Lordships

Special frontier authorities.

Characteristics:

- Military autonomy
- Defensive responsibilities
- Reduced bureaucracy

Benefits:

- Strong defense
- Rapid response

Risks:

- Growing independence
- Ambitious commanders

---

# Church Territories

Certain territories may be governed by religious institutions.

Examples:

- Prince-Bishoprics
- Monastic Territories
- Ecclesiastical Regions

Benefits:

- Stability
- Legitimacy

Risks:

- Reduced secular control

---

# Dynastic Holdings

Dynastic Holdings represent territories administered by family members.

Examples:

- Younger Sons
- Cadet Branches
- Trusted Relatives

Benefits:

- Family prestige
- Dynasty development
- Long-term loyalty

Risks:

- Succession disputes
- Cadet branch growth
- Rival claims

---

# Regional Authority

Every region possesses an Authority Score.

Measures:

- Acceptance of governance
- Administrative effectiveness
- Stability

Authority increases through:

- Competent leadership
- Successful governance
- Development

Authority decreases through:

- Corruption
- Weak governors
- Rebellion
- War

---

# Crown Authority

Represents overall central control.

Range:

0 – 100

---

## High Authority

Characteristics:

- Strong central government
- Greater obedience
- Higher administrative control

Risks:

- Noble resentment
- Regional resistance

---

## Medium Authority

Characteristics:

- Balance between center and regions

Often the most stable setting.

---

## Low Authority

Characteristics:

- Powerful regional leaders
- Greater autonomy

Risks:

- Fragmentation
- Weak coordination

---

# Expansion Pressure

Realm Governance responds directly to expansion.

Small Realm:

- Direct rule is practical

Medium Realm:

- Governed regions become useful

Large Realm:

- Delegation becomes necessary

Expansion naturally increases administrative pressure.

# Design Constraints

Realm Governance should remain understandable.

Players manage:

- Important Regions
- Important Characters
- Important Decisions

Players should not manage:

- Hundreds of minor vassals
- Endless paperwork
- Excessive bureaucracy

The system should provide medieval political depth without overwhelming complexity.

# Notes

Realm Governance serves as the bridge between:

Dynasties

↓

Succession

↓

Territorial Administration

↓

Politics

↓

History

Total War underrepresents governance.

Crusader Kings often overrepresents governance.

Medieval War seeks a balance between both.

The purpose is not to manage every noble.

The purpose is to manage the people and regions that actually matter.

As realms grow, governance becomes increasingly important.

Power may win territory.

Governance determines whether that territory remains part of the realm.
