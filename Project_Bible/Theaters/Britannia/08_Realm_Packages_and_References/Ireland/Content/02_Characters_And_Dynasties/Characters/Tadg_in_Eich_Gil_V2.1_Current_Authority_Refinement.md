## Historical Identity

Tadg in Eich Gil, also represented as Tadc in Eich Gil Ua Conchobair, was a Síl Muiredaig ruler of Connacht and the father of Áed in Gai Bernaig. He succeeded to the overkingship around 1010, ruled for roughly twenty years, and was killed in 1030.

His reign included conflict with northern and internal rivals, interaction with Bréifne, pressure from Donnchad mac Briain, and intervention beyond Connacht. His death interrupted the line's possession of the overkingship. Áed's later accession in 1046 is therefore interpreted as restoration rather than uninterrupted inheritance.

Tadg is not a living person in the January 1062 campaign. He cannot be instantiated as an active ruler, adviser, commander, courtier, claimant, or AI decision-maker.

## Historical Uncertainty

### Strongly supported

- Tadg belonged to Síl Muiredaig, the lineage later associated with the Uí Conchobair.
- He was a son of Cathal, a ruler of Connacht.
- He succeeded to the overkingship around 1010.
- He ruled until his death in 1030.
- He yielded hostages to Donnchad mac Briain in 1025.
- He was the father of Áed in Gai Bernaig.

### Disputed or uncertain

- The precise agents and political responsibility for his killing.
- The exact meaning and duration of the 1025 hostage settlement.
- The complete number and identity of his children.
- Complex later marital history associated with Derbfhorgaill.
- The exact scope and interpretation of particular campaigns and rivalries.

### Project baseline

Tadg is a deceased legacy figure. His importance in 1062 exists through descent, evidence, dynastic and regional memory, restoration narratives, and political invocation by living audiences. The project does not treat every later account as fact or every audience as sharing the same memory.

## Legacy Function

Tadg's primary legacy function is to provide part of the historical and genealogical basis for Áed in Gai Bernaig's restored kingship and for Síl Muiredaig or Uí Conchobair continuity. His death creates a remembered rupture between an earlier kingship and Áed's later restoration.

His legacy may inform:

- dynastic descent and genealogy;
- narratives of interrupted succession and restoration;
- Connacht reception of Áed's rule;
- memories of conflict with Bréifne;
- caution concerning Munster pressure and hostage demands;
- warnings about involvement in central Irish politics;
- commemorations, assemblies, genealogical recitations, or rival narratives.

Legacy does not grant title possession, military capacity, current recognition, automatic hostility, or government performance to descendants.

## Evidence and Narrative Boundary

The profile distinguishes:

- **Historical fact:** Tadg lived, ruled, died in 1030, and was Áed's father, within the project's confidence bounds.
- **Evidence:** Annalistic notices, genealogies, hostage references, and other surviving records, owned by Historical Memory.
- **Narrative:** Interpretations of Tadg as defender, fallen king, victim, failed central intervener, or source of restoration legitimacy.
- **Reception:** Different views held by Áed's household, Uí Conchobair supporters, Bréifne audiences, Munster rulers, Mide actors, and religious institutions.
- **Legacy:** Strategic use of those memories after Tadg's death.

Narrative cannot alter the campaign fact record. A genealogy can support reception of a claim but cannot create title ownership. A later epithet or commemorative image cannot act as an opening Trait.

### Uí Conchobair and Síl Muiredaig supporters

Dynastic audiences may use Tadg to support continuity, cohesion, claimant reception, or expectations of rule. They may also remember the succession interruption as a warning against weak transfer arrangements.
