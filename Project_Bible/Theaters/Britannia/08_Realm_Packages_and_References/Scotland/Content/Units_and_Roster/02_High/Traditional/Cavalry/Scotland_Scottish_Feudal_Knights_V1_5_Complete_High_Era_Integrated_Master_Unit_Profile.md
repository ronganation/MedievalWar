## 1. Strong Unit Verdict

**Scottish Feudal Knights are Scotland's proposed mail-armored lance cavalry: a costly knightly following that gathers for a coherent mounted impact, attacks an exposed or already occupied enemy, and turns a successful first collision into a short decisive fight before recovering its formation.**

Their signature is **Make the First Contact Count**. March Riders preserve response capacity. Mormaer's Horsemen supply selected mounted contact. These knights concentrate more of their proposed equipment, practice, and cost on delivering a formed lance attack.

The card is not a universal infantry breaker. A prepared spear front, denied approach, fresh mounted reserve, or exhausted horses can defeat the intended action. Mail protects riders; it does not armor every horse or remove the consequences of entanglement.

## 2. One-Screen Total War Card

- **Name:** Scottish Feudal Knights.
- **Epithet:** **The Mail-Armored Lance Strike**.
- **Era:** Conditional High-era knightly development.
- **Signature:** **Make the First Contact Count**.
- **Class:** Mail-armored shock cavalry.
- **Promise:** A powerful formed lance attack when the army creates a usable approach and a vulnerable target.
- **Best use:** Enemy flanks fixed by Scottish foot, weak advancing wings, exposed missiles, and mounted counterattacks with room to form.
- **Strengths:** Concentrated impact, substantial proposed rider protection, practiced mounted weapon use, and useful short melee after contact.
- **Weaknesses:** Prepared spears, narrow or broken approaches, concentrated missiles, fresh reserves, costly mount losses, and prolonged encirclement.
- **Campaign bargain:** Narrower, more expensive knightly service with demanding horse and equipment recovery.
- **Instruction:** Form before impact; do not spend the lance attack on ground that cannot deliver it.
- **Flavor:** **A clear approach. A fixed enemy. One contact worth the cost.**

## 3. Player-Facing Description

Scottish Feudal Knights bring a different mounted tradition into the Scottish host: mail, shields, lances, and the collective practice needed to turn an approach into a coherent attack. They are most dangerous when friendly infantry occupies the enemy and leaves a flank open to a formed charge.

They remain expensive people on vulnerable horses. A blocked approach can squander the impact before it arrives, while fresh spears or a mounted reserve can turn a promising attack into a costly melee. Keep the formation together, strike a target that matters, and recover it before the first advantage disappears.

### The Mounted Decision of a Kingdom of Many Hosts

**The host holds the enemy's attention. The knights spend the opening.** This is authored game flavor, not a historical quotation. Scotland's knightly force should feel potent when committed, not permanently inferior to every foreign knight. Its asymmetric burden is the concentration of value in fewer suitable riders, horses, equipment packages, and service networks.

The player fantasy is to preserve a visible mounted reserve, recognize an opening worth its cost, bring enough lances into contact together, and decide whether the immediate advantage justifies remaining. The target need not already be routing. Suitable ordinary infantry, an exposed missile body, a committed enemy wing, or a dangerous mounted attack can all justify the action.

A clean charge should create a satisfying local decision. It should not promise that every lance hits, every horse survives, or every opponent breaks. The player is buying the possibility of a decisive mounted intervention, with a campaign consequence if that possibility is wasted.

## 11. Horse and Sustainment Envelope

Mounts must support the intended rider, equipment, and combat workload. No modern breed, exact size, or universal heavy warhorse is assigned. A costly mount is not automatically immune to missiles, bad ground, or exhaustion.

Only real overlapping assignments compete: escort, household protection, transport, and other cavalry cannot use the same rider or mount simultaneously. Do not impose a generic penalty on unrelated sources.

## 13. Battlefield Loop

### Reserve

Hold where a useful approach is reachable. Do not exhaust horses circling for a perfect target that never appears.

### Align

Gather enough order and frontage. A narrow lane may deliver only a few leading riders, wasting the formation's intended impact.

### Approach

Choose the actual route, not just the target icon. Friendly troops, obstacles, slope, and enemy reserves can invalidate it.

### Impact

Commit against an exposed or occupied target. The first collision is an opportunity, not proof that the enemy must break.

### Exploit or Separate

Continue a short fight if the local result remains favorable. If the enemy holds or receives support, seek separation before the exit closes.

### Reform

Gather survivors, restore order, assess weapons and horses, then decide whether another attack is justified.

## 14. Impact Economy and Player Skill

The decisive resource is **a formed lance attack delivered while the target and ground still make it valuable**.

A longer approach can improve alignment but arrive after the opening closes. A fast improvised attack can save a crisis but deliver less mass. Continuing after impact may finish the enemy or lose expensive horses to fresh support.

The card should be satisfying, not an expensive spectator. A good formed flank attack against vulnerable troops should deliver a clear combat advantage in testing. The design does not require every target to be routing before knights may attack.

Repeated charges remain possible after real separation and reforming. No artificial ban on cycle charging is imposed, but no free damage loop or instant lance reset is granted.

### The Commitment Window: Fun, Risk, and a Real Second Action

The unit must not become an expensive spectator waiting for an impossible perfect charge. A sufficiently clear approach and suitable target can justify commitment even when some risk remains. The meaningful choice is whether this opening changes the battle enough to spend horse condition, weapons, and reserve availability.

```yaml
commitment_choices:
  Wait:
    gain: PreserveConditionAndThreatenSeveralPossibleTargets
    cost: OpeningMayCloseAndFriendlyInfantryMaySuffer
  Commit:
    gain: ConcentrateMountedImpactAtAUsefulDecision
    cost: SpendReservePositionHorseConditionAndEquipmentReadiness
  Abort:
    gain: PreserveCompanyWhenApproachOrTargetFailsBeforeContact
    cost: LostTimePositionAndPossibleExposure
  Exploit:
    gain: FinishAnImmediateLocalAdvantage
    cost: RisingEntanglementAndFreshReserveRisk
  Separate:
    gain: PreserveRidersHorsesAndFutureThreat
    cost: EnemyMayRecoverOrRetainTheGround
```

The signature is not a one-use-per-battle limitation. A second or later charge is allowed when real separation, reforming, horse condition, leadership, weapon state, and another valuable approach support it. Equally, repeatedly clicking away and back does not restore lances, fit horses, or formation order.

The sword phase is meaningful combat, not helpless waiting after a lance breaks. Mail-protected riders can exploit disorder and defeat vulnerable opponents in a short fight. They should nevertheless lose their favorable bargain when trapped among fresh, supported infantry or opposing mounted reserves. This is a mission and geometry ceiling, not an arbitrary countdown that makes every knight ineffective after a fixed interval.

## 16. Matchups and Counterplay

Preferred opportunities are exposed missiles, weak or disordered infantry wings, a flank fixed by the Spear Host, and local mounted opposition where order and support favor the knights. Ordinary infantry without prepared spear reception can be a legitimate target; outcome remains conditional on the battle state.

Prepared spears, dense supported infantry, stronger fresh cavalry, narrow crossings, and concentrated missiles are poor assignments. An opponent can anchor a flank, retain a reserve, obstruct the approach, or force an early commitment.

Mail improves the rider's proposed protection, not the horse's universal survival. A stalled attack can become costly even when the knights win individual close fights. No nationality or social-rank victory multiplier is proposed.

## 22. Veterans and Rebuilt Knights

Understrength veterans can retain better approach judgment, alignment, post-impact control, and separation timing. They deliver less contact mass and may depend heavily on surviving leaders.

A rebuilt formation can regain people and equipment before restoring collective mounted practice. Replacement horses, unfamiliar riders, and uneven lance readiness can make the first attack less coherent despite full headcount.

Experience does not create mail, suitable mounts, knightly status, or political access. Improvement can remain within the same family when the source and battlefield promise persist.

## 30B. Broad Naming Review and Final Decision

### Scottish Feudal Knights

**Selected.** This is the best mature-roster name because it identifies Scotland, communicates the knightly battlefield family, and points to the land-for-service military development supported by the broader twelfth-century context. It does not require every rider to be Norman, Anglo-Norman, Breton, Flemish, or native-born.

### Scoto-Norman Knights

**Retired as the primary display name.** It is flavorful but too narrow. It risks turning a changing Scottish service institution into an ethnic unit label and can wrongly suggest that local adoption or mixed service networks are outside the family.

### Scottish Mailed Knights

**Retained only as a possible earlier or reduced equipment expression.** It describes visible armor, not the institutional distinction. It is also too generic to carry a mature faction identity by itself.

### King's Knights

**Rejected for this card.** It would imply crown-household ownership and collide with a future explicitly royal mounted unit. Feudal service and royal household service are not identical.

### Lowland Knights

**Not selected.** It might become useful for a specific source relationship, but the retrieved evidence does not establish a universal Lowland-only family boundary.
