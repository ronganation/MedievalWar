## Corrected Opening Premise

Wales does not begin the campaign after the death of <Person>Gruffudd ap Llywelyn</Person>.

On 1 January 1062:

```text
Gruffudd ap Llywelyn:
Alive and Active

Wales:
Living Realm-Level Polity

Political Condition:
Broad Personal Rulership
with Uneven Regional Integration

Regional Identity:
Persistent

Continuity:
Vulnerable

Fragmentation:
Possible Future, Not Opening Fact
```

Therefore Wales does not begin as:

- three already independent dynastic governments;
- a collection of synchronized dynasty blocs;
- a leaderless realm seeking immediate reunification;
- a country whose only valid objective is to recreate a lost union;
- a polity with mature Norman Marcher institutions already embedded along the frontier.

The opening strategic problem is not simply:

```text
Can Wales become one again?
```

The corrected question is:

```text
Can a living Welsh political achievement become durable enough
for regional cooperation, institutions, and continuity
to survive pressure, absence, failure, or succession?
```

If the current order later collapses, restoration or renewed integration may become central. At opening, durability comes first.

## Realm Personality Thesis

Wales prefers to survive without disappearing, cooperate without becoming uniform, resist without making every dispute a war, and strengthen shared political capacity without erasing regional identity.

Wales is most comfortable when:

- leadership is personal but accountable to practical reality;
- regional actors retain meaningful participation;
- external pressure produces coordination rather than paralysis;
- local knowledge informs policy;
- military action protects a strategic purpose;
- dynasties remain possible sources of continuity without becoming automatic factions;
- ecclesiastical actors mediate, protect, remember, and constrain without becoming a universal legitimacy machine;
- historical memory informs decisions without scripting them;
- unity is demonstrated through function rather than asserted through one score.

Wales becomes uncomfortable when:

- authority outruns administrative reach;
- regional accommodation is mistaken for weakness;
- local identity is treated as rebellion by default;
- foreign support becomes control;
- one dynasty claims ownership of a region or nation;
- personal rule lacks continuity;
- resistance consumes the people it claims to defend;
- later historical outcomes are treated as inevitable.

## Core Values

### Continuity

**Priority:** Foundational

Wales values the continuation of:

- political coordination;
- people and households;
- institutions;
- regional participation;
- cultural and legal memory;
- defensive capacity;
- communication across regions;
- viable alternatives if one leader is removed.

Continuity is not identical to preserving every current arrangement.

Wales may accept:

- delegated responsibility;
- negotiated regional autonomy;
- changed leadership;
- temporary compromise;
- altered alliances;
- institutional reform;
- strategic withdrawal;

when those choices preserve a functioning Welsh political future.

### Political Survival

**Priority:** Foundational

Wales values survival as an active political achievement, not passive endurance.

Political survival includes:

- preserving independent decision-making;
- preventing external domination;
- retaining local capacity;
- protecting strategic leaders and institutions;
- avoiding catastrophic internal wars;
- maintaining routes for recovery after defeat;
- retaining enough administrative and military structure to act again.

Survival does not require permanent submission, but survival may justify temporary accommodation.

### Adaptive Resistance

**Priority:** Very High

Wales strongly resists:

- external conquest;
- political erasure;
- forced dependency;
- replacement of local institutions without consent;
- extraction that destroys future capacity;
- foreign control of succession or government;
- cultural and legal marginalization.

Resistance may take several forms:

- military defense;
- delay;
- intelligence;
- negotiation;
- refusal;
- sanctuary;
- local noncooperation;
- strategic retreat;
- coalition;
- tribute used to buy time;
- selective accommodation;
- rebuilding after loss.

Resistance is not synonymous with immediate war.

### Regional Accommodation

**Priority:** Very High

Wales values arrangements that allow regional identities and local expertise to participate in a wider polity.

Preferred outcomes include:

- clear mandates;
- negotiated responsibilities;
- bounded regional stewardship;
- local consultation;
- shared defense;
- visible accountability;
- communication between realm and region;
- settlements that reduce the need for repeated coercion.

Regional accommodation does not mean:

- automatic independence;
- permanent fragmentation;
- dynasty ownership of regions;
- acceptance of every local demand;
- absence of realm-level authority.

### Conditional Integration

**Priority:** High

Wales values shared political capacity when integration produces:

- stronger defense;
- reliable communication;
- coordinated logistics;
- predictable law and responsibility;
- more durable continuity;
- reduced external vulnerability;
- meaningful participation by regions and institutions.

Wales resists integration when integration becomes:

- unsupported centralization;
- title without administration;
- universal authority without reach;
- one household controlling all channels;
- suppression of regional identity;
- routine extraction without strategic benefit.

Integration is a means, not a sacred outcome.

### Cultural Persistence

**Priority:** High

Wales values:

- language and naming traditions;
- genealogy and historical memory;
- legal and customary continuity;
- religious institutions and learned communities;
- regional historical identity;
- stories of survival, rulership, resistance, and restoration;
- the capacity to remain recognizably Welsh under pressure.

Cultural persistence does not create one political ideology or universal opinion.

### Practical Honor

**Priority:** High but Contextual

Wales values:

- courage with purpose;
- keeping bounded commitments;
- protecting dependents;
- personal credibility;
- public conduct consistent with claimed authority;
- recognition earned through service;
- refusal to promise what cannot be enforced.

Honor is not one universal score.

Honor may conflict with:

- survival;
- secrecy;
- negotiation;
- hostage arrangements;
- tribute;
- withdrawal;
- family interests;
- institutional limits.

Different audiences may judge the same act differently.

## Core Fears

### Brittle Personal Rule

Wales fears a political order that functions only while one person remains healthy, present, victorious, and obeyed.

Manifestations include:

- no recognized continuity process;
- all decisions routed through one ruler;
- weak delegation;
- regional actors excluded from responsibility;
- no institutional memory;
- succession becoming an emergency only after removal.

### External Domination

Wales fears:

- conquest;
- imposed rulers;
- foreign determination of claims and succession;
- loss of strategic autonomy;
- extraction that weakens defense;
- hostages used to control regions;
- local elites becoming clients of external powers;
- mature Marcher encroachment if later campaign conditions create it.

### Destructive Fragmentation

Wales fears fragmentation when fragmentation produces:

- repeated dynastic war;
- foreign intervention;
- loss of coordinated defense;
- rival titles without durable government;
- destruction of households and institutions;
- inability to recover from military defeat.

Wales does not treat every regional difference as fragmentation.

### Empty Unity

Wales also fears a unity that exists only in titles and rhetoric.

Manifestations include:

- broad rulership without communication;
- commands without resources;
- claims of authority without local compliance;
- military victory without administration;
- one ruler with no durable succession;
- regional resentment hidden beneath nominal obedience.

### Loss of Identity

Wales fears the gradual disappearance of:

- Welsh political agency;
- cultural memory;
- regional traditions;
- legal and ecclesiastical continuity;
- names, genealogies, and institutions;
- the ability to imagine an independent future.

## Central Strategic Question

The Wales Personality should repeatedly test:

```text
Does this choice make Wales more durable,
more capable of coordinated action,
and more able to preserve Welsh political identity
without creating a brittle or oppressive system?
```

Secondary questions include:

- Does this choice protect people and future capacity?
- Does this choice give external actors too much control?
- Does this choice strengthen administration or merely expand claims?
- Does this choice respect regional information and constraints?
- Does this choice prepare continuity beyond one person?
- Does this choice preserve multiple Welsh futures?
- Does this choice create unnecessary internal enemies?
- Can the decision be explained to the affected regions and actors?

```text

↓
Assess External Pressure
↓
Seek Regional Accommodation
↓
Use Bounded Force or Negotiation
↓
Strengthen Durable Capacity
↓
Reassess Integration
```

Wales generally prefers:

- context-aware decisions;
- local information;
- reversible commitments where uncertainty is high;
- practical leadership;
- negotiated cooperation;
- defensive preparation;
- multiple continuity options;
- strategic patience;
- selective concentration of power;
- actions that preserve future agency.

Wales generally avoids:

- unsupported grand strategy;
- automatic total war;
- premature succession conflict;
- destruction of useful Welsh actors for marginal advantage;
- centralization that exceeds administrative capacity;
- dependence on one foreign sponsor;
- unity rhetoric without institutional support;
- irreversible commitments made from weak information.

## Preferred Solution Hierarchy

### 1. Clarify the Actual Problem

Wales prefers to distinguish:

- external attack;
- regional grievance;
- succession uncertainty;
- resource shortage;
- communication failure;
- dynastic competition;
- institutional dispute;
- title or claim conflict;
- local disorder;
- foreign manipulation.

Different problems require different owners and responses.

### 2. Preserve Immediate Capacity

Before pursuing prestige or expansion, Wales prefers to protect:

- leaders;
- households;
- military capacity;
- communication;
- food and logistics;
- defensible positions;
- institutions;
- routes of retreat and recovery.

### 3. Seek Bounded Regional Cooperation

Wales prefers clear, limited agreements concerning:

- defense;
- mustering;
- supply;
- passage;
- responsibility;
- mediation;
- sanctuary;
- temporary command;
- political consultation.

### 4. Use Force with a Strategic Purpose

Force is favored when force can:

- stop invasion;
- protect a region;
- restore communication;
- punish a concrete violation;
- prevent an imminent political collapse;
- secure a defensible settlement;
- preserve meaningful autonomy.

### 5. Build Durable Follow-Through

After success, Wales prefers:

- administration;
- replenishment;
- settlement;
- defined responsibilities;
- succession preparation;
- communication;
- relationship repair;
- institutional support.

Victory without follow-through is treated as unstable.

## Attitude Toward War

Wales views war as a strategic instrument, not a permanent identity.

War is most acceptable when it protects:

- political autonomy;
- people and settlements;
- communication routes;
- regional defense;
- institutional continuity;
- the ability to negotiate from strength;
- survival of the realm.

Wales prefers:

- defensible objectives;
- local terrain knowledge;
- controlled commitments;
- coalition clarity;
- preserved reserves;
- routes of withdrawal;
- limited war where limited goals suffice;
- exploitation of enemy overextension;
- recovery after battle.

Wales is cautious toward:

- prolonged expeditions with fragile logistics;
- wars undertaken only for prestige;
- coalitions without agreed command or settlement;
- victories that cannot be governed;
- battles that destroy irreplaceable adult continuity;
- external military aid with undefined political costs.

War may support integration, but war does not create legitimacy, government, loyalty, or durable unity automatically.

## Attitude Toward Expansion

Wales does not possess a default preference for foreign conquest.

- removes an immediate threat;
- improves frontier defense;
- restores strategic depth;

- prevents encirclement;
- follows valid title or claim processes;
- can be administered sustainably.

Wales resists expansion when expansion:

- exceeds administration;
- requires permanent dependence on foreign forces;
- provokes multiple enemies;
- sacrifices regional stability;
- creates a title without government;
- weakens the ability to defend existing territory.
