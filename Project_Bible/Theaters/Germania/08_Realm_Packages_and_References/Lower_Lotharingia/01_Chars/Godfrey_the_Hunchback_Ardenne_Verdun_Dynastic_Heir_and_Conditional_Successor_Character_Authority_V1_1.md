## 3. Core character thesis

**The adult dynastic son who must become politically credible without being allowed to inherit his father's identity, coalition, or future by default.**

## 10. Personality and decision tendencies

These are authoring directions, not hidden emotional claims.

### 10.1 Values

- recognition as a person rather than an extension of the father;
- dynastic continuity supported by material preparation;
- assignments that build competence and reputation;
- household security and an independent support base;
- settlements that clarify expectations;
- marriage negotiations that preserve personal and dynastic viability;
- avoiding promises made in his name without access or resources.

### 10.2 Restraints

- resist automatic identification with all of the father's enemies and allies;
- avoid command without loyal contingents and supply;
- avoid inheriting undeliverable coalition promises;
- distinguish expected succession from recognized installation;
- avoid becoming a hostage or marriage instrument without meaningful terms;
- retain an exit from simultaneous Lotharingian and Tuscan obligations.

### 10.3 Decision method

Godfrey should ask what authority is actually granted, which household supports the action, what resources exist, which audiences will recognize the result, and whether the decision creates independent standing or merely transfers the father's liabilities.

## 25. Player-facing meaning

The younger Godfrey gives the Lower Lotharingian struggle a genuine next generation. The player can cultivate, contain, divide, negotiate with, employ, protect, or neglect the dynastic son. The player-facing fantasy is not waiting for a historical inheritance date. It is deciding whether the person who might inherit the restoration project becomes capable of carrying it.

Ordinary turns should rarely surface more than one major Godfrey decision. Pressure rises during the father's absence, military preparation, marriage negotiations, coalition conflict, or succession crisis.
