The retrieved Germania inventory places German Foot Knights after Saxon Axe Veterans in its direct sequence and identifies the card as High Era heavy armored contract infantry. It is not an Early Era opening addition. No existing foot-knight predecessor was retrieved or claimed.

## 1. Strong Unit Verdict: Stand and Break

**German Foot Knights are premium hired heavy infantry who meet a hard close fight behind shields, remain dangerous under pressure, and use one-handed maces to defeat the protected enemy foot that ordinary contract infantry struggles to finish.** They buy the employer a dependable point of heavy melee, not a slower copy of Saxon Axe Veterans.

Their signature remains **Stand and Break**. They enter in order, establish guarded contact, survive the enemy's first credible answer, and convert that protection into repeated mace pressure until the opposing sector yields or the commander judges the price too high. They can lead a supported attack, counterattack committed enemy foot, relieve a failing heavy-melee sector, or hold a contested objective. They are not passive anvils and they are not confined to waiting for another unit to create the victory.

The pleasure is a hard enemy formation finally losing ground against troops that can stay in the fight. Their maces give the card a protected-target emphasis; shields and heavy equipment make that offense more defensible than an exposed two-handed assault. This is not automatic armor penetration, invulnerability, or a guaranteed win against every knightly opponent.

The price is substantial. These troops cost heavily to hire and retain, consume a scarce pool, and are expensive to restore. Short weapons cannot replace spear reach. A long approach, crossfire, deep prepared polearms, cavalry impact, and attacks on both shoulders can defeat them. The army buys a powerful heavy-infantry tool, not permission to stop using combined arms.

## 3. Encyclopedia and Recruitment Text

Some hired companies are bought to fill a line. German Foot Knights are bought for the place where that line must not give way, or where an enemy too well equipped for ordinary foot must finally be driven back. Heavy protection and maintained shields let them enter a serious infantry contest with more confidence than lightly equipped contract troops. Their maces make the purpose of that close fight clear.

They do not need a spectacular charge to justify their wages. Once in contact, a formed company can keep its shields working, answer pressure, and deliver repeated short attacks. A commander may use them to anchor a contested street, attack beside a spear line, or meet enemy heavy infantry already committed against tired friendly troops. Their best result is not merely surviving: it is winning useful ground with enough order left to keep it.

Their equipment does not make them untouchable. Long weapons can deny entry, missiles can punish an exposed approach, and cavalry can turn a slow recovery into disaster. A narrow lane may favor their compact weapons while also trapping the company against fresh defenders. Even a successful fight can cost more in experienced men and maintained equipment than the employer expected.

The contract provides ready heavy infantry through a finite hiring market. It does not provide an inexhaustible household army. A company that survives several campaigns can become worth retaining, but high wages continue when the battle is over and lost experience cannot simply be purchased back.

**Recruitment text:** Heavily equipped hired foot with maces and active shields. Strong in hard infantry contests and counterattacks; costly, short-ranged, and vulnerable to isolation.

This is authored game text, not a claim that a documented regiment used this exact English name.

## 4. Name and What Foot Knights Means

Keep **German Foot Knights** as the working display name. It connects directly to the inventory and supplied Total War reference. “German” describes the proposed hiring expression, not a political owner or a national combat bonus.

“Foot Knights” is a player-facing heavy-infantry label. The card does not require every modeled fighter to possess a legally verified knightly title. A represented company may include high-status paid fighters and compatible well-equipped retainers meeting its battlefield standard. Exact social composition remains a research question rather than an invented census.

The unit is hired for foot service. It is not a cavalry card that has lost its horses, and hiring it does not secretly create a reserve stable. If a future shared dismount system lets mounted troops fight on foot, that remains a separate service state of the actual mounted company.

No famous captain, royal household, religious order, or Imperial command allegiance is assigned. Employers obtain service through the mercenary system; they do not obtain political ownership of a German institution.

## 6. Protected Pressure Rather Than Another Axe Card

The Saxon veterans' Hew the Line identity centers on dangerous two-handed offense after delivery. German Foot Knights center on **guarded pressure through a hard exchange**. They can still attack; the distinction is how they sustain the contest, not a prohibition on forward movement.

Against ordinary infantry, both premium cards must be useful. The foot knights pay for protection, active shields, and a more secure fighting posture. The axe veterans retain a stronger exposed assault expression and should not become pointless merely because a later company is available.

Against well-protected foot, the mace card has a proposed relative efficiency advantage over ordinary cutting infantry. That advantage is a design calibration target, not a historical formula. It must not be combined with the best charge, best reach, best defense, cheapest price, and fastest movement.

A household title alone does not decide the matchup. Actual protection, weapons, skill, strength, condition, and support do. An exceptionally well-equipped domestic company can outlast these hires; a poorly supported household formation can still lose to them.

## 9. Equipment Evolution Without Backdating Plate

The supplied mod offers its plate-armored mace company after 1280. This profile does not adopt that as an independently validated date for a complete equipment package.

The proposed earlier expression is mail-centered with dated reinforcement, active shields, and a suitable helmet. A later expression can improve torso, limb, and head protection as supported by research. The company remains one family unless a materially different posture, source, or role justifies a separate card.

Do not assemble the earliest package by taking the most protective item from several later centuries. Helmet, torso defense, limb protection, shield, and clothing must be reviewed together. A late visor does not become early because the rest of the model wears mail.

Better equipment requires actual access, cost, repair support, and eligible companies. A research unlock changes eligibility; it does not issue free armor or create trained people. Existing companies do not instantly transform when the calendar advances.

## 11. Mace and Shield Contact Contract

The intended fighting posture is one hand attacking while the shield protects and recovers. Shield facing, weapon access, footing, and fatigue should matter through the shared battle model.

A mace strike is not a guaranteed armor bypass, stun, knockdown, or instant incapacitation. The proposed protected-target value should come from a **smaller loss of effectiveness against protection than ordinary cutting weapons experience**, expressed through the shared weapon and protection model. If that model uses an armor-piercing component, the component must be bounded: armor still matters, shields still intercept by facing, and unprotected targets do not become strangely resistant simply because the mace is tuned for heavy opponents.

The shield cannot cover the flank and rear while the fighter faces forward. A reaching attack, forced turn, or lost grip can reduce protection. Good equipment and veteran handling make the company more reliable, not physically impossible to outmaneuver.

Compact weapon actions can remain useful in closer spaces than wide two-handed swings. Extreme compression still prevents effective fighting. The card does not gain free attacks through friendly bodies or a bonus merely because a street is narrow.

## 12. Stand and Break: Full Battle Rhythm

**Approach in order:** use shields, terrain, and support to reach a worthwhile fight without exhausting the company. Heavy protection permits some risk; it does not justify an unlimited missile walk.

**Establish contact:** use a short charge or deliberate advance. The company must be useful without a long run-up. It can meet an enemy attack and answer it.

**Work the contest:** maintain guarded short-weapon pressure. The goal is to defeat or displace the opposing infantry, not simply accumulate survival time. The company should have enough offense to justify premium wages.

**Read the response:** distinguish a yielding enemy, a hard but productive contest, and a stalled attack being reinforced by fresh reserves. Protected does not mean cheap to lose.

**Gain and secure:** advance into actual space and keep the shoulders connected. Holding a won gate or street can be the successful end of the task.

**Relieve or recover:** halt, accept support, or withdraw where possible. No order instantly restores fatigue, casualties, or damaged equipment.

Stand and Break is a descriptive identity using ordinary orders, not a cooldown, magical stance, or mandatory sequence of special buttons.

## 14. Charge, Holding, and Counterattack

The charge is useful but not the main purchase. A short entry can seize initiative; repeated guarded attacks carry the company's value after impact.

A defensive start is not a penalty state. Against suitable infantry, holding a manageable front and counterattacking an overextended enemy can be an excellent use. The unit should not need repeated charge cycling to maintain worthwhile output.

The strongest reserve use is to meet a committed enemy before the friendly sector collapses. A fresh heavy company can stabilize the contest and then drive it back. That is a tactical possibility, not an automatic line-restoration effect.

A failed attack can settle into a costly holding fight. The commander must decide whether the objective warrants continued losses or whether the company should be relieved. The unit's protection buys time for that decision, not a free exit.

Understrength companies shorten their frontage or lose depth. Good equipment cannot occupy missing ground. Rear ranks can replace losses where space allows; they cannot strike at full effect through occupied friendly models.

## 16. Leadership, Morale, and Company Quality

A captain and subordinate leaders keep the company facing the right threat, control entry, maintain the front, halt pursuit, and organize recovery. Their value is practical control, not a supernatural aura.

The baseline is an established high-grade company. Further experience improves judgment and collective timing. Heavy replacements may restore strength before they restore the same quality.

Morale should be steady under ordinary pressure. No automatic cowardice penalty is attached to being hired, and no unbreakable status is attached to the word knight. Encirclement, leader loss, fatigue, casualties, and army collapse remain serious.

Leader loss can delay orders and weaken rallying without instantly routing a healthy company. A surviving captain cannot make an exhausted isolated remnant fight as if its original strength remains.

## 17. Fatigue, Mobility, and the Cost of Staying

Heavy equipment and repeated close combat should make freshness valuable. The company is capable of a substantial fight, not a unit with an arbitrary short timer before it becomes useless.

Long running, hills, mud, repeated turns, and sustained pressure reduce attack rhythm, shield recovery, and reform. The player should see a gradual loss of effectiveness rather than a hidden class penalty.

Battlefield speed remains that of equipped foot. Do not make the company absurdly slow merely to compensate for strong armor. Its real costs are money, scarce recovery, short reach, exposure, and condition.

A pause can recover some fatigue under shared rules. It does not repair armor, heal wounds, or restore dead leaders. Moving a reserve repeatedly across the field spends condition even before it fights.

## 18. Missiles and Directional Protection

Active shields and heavy protection should make the company more comfortable under a comparable frontal approach than unshielded heavy axemen, subject to actual equipment and projectile tuning. That is a proposed relationship, not universal immunity.

Crossfire, rear shots, close javelins, strong crossbows, and attacks during turning can remain dangerous. A dense formation offers more mutual shelter but also a concentrated target.

The correct response is ordinary support: suppress the enemy, shorten the exposed route, use terrain, screen where practical, or accept losses for a worthwhile contact. A screen does not create a magical projectile barrier.

Friendly missiles must cease or shift when the melee masks their lane. The company does not become transparent because its target is valuable. Autoresolve must also preserve the difference between guarded delivery and prolonged crossfire.

## 19. Cavalry and Polearm Counterplay

A mace and shield are not a prepared spear front. The company receives no spear brace or universal anti-large multiplier. Fresh cavalry with room to charge is a serious threat, especially against a flank, rear, or disordered withdrawal.

Stalled riders can be vulnerable in close combat. The company may fight them effectively under favorable conditions without becoming the army's preferred mounted receiver.

Long weapons can deny short-weapon entry. Deep prepared pikes should remain an unattractive frontal target. Ordinary spears can also impose a real entry cost, even if the foot knights are dangerous after reaching workable contact.

Counterplay should be readable: preserve reach, pin and flank, force repeated turns, use crossfire, commit a fresh reserve, or threaten the exit. No hidden national penalty is needed to make the unit beatable.

## 20. Passage, Relief, and Withdrawal

A neighboring attack is often safer than trying to move through a pinned friendly line. A real interval, open shoulder, or deliberate withdrawal can admit the foot knights.

Imperfect relief remains possible. Some files may enter while others are delayed. The shared movement model should not demand a pristine ritual, but it must not allow model phasing or an instant perfect handoff.

Withdrawal requires a direction, time, space, and manageable enemy pressure. Shields can support a guarded recovery where the shared model allows; they do not grant free disengagement.

A company may win the local melee and still be unable to leave safely if cavalry or congestion closes the return lane. Preserve the distinction between combat success and a good overall commitment.

## 21. Siege, Streets, and Objective Fighting

This is a strong proposed card for **foothold consolidation and heavy objective fighting**: widening a gate entry, defeating a defending reserve, taking a courtyard, or holding a contested street. Compact weapons and shields support the identity without creating a blanket siege bonus. Compared with Saxon Axe Veterans, the foot knights are the safer choice for a prolonged contested foothold; compared with spear companies, they are the stronger close-melee choice after enemy cavalry access and long weapon lanes have been reduced.

The company may lead a manageable assault when the tactical conditions justify it. It is not automatically a second-wave-only unit. Conversely, a ladder, narrow stair, rubble choke, or missile-swept wall can prevent its equipment and formation from delivering value.

Heavy protection is useful but expensive to spend. A gate won at severe cost may still be a good result; repeated unsupported storming should not be the default employment.

No wall demolition, special gate-breaking power, engineering skill, or free garrison upkeep is assigned. Ordinary shared siege and economy rules remain in control.

## 23. Whole-Battle Employment in Germania

**Deploy:** place the company at a likely heavy-infantry decision point, behind a threatened sector, or beside a spear shoulder. Keep enough frontage and return space for it to work.

**Shape the approach:** use missiles and terrain to make contact affordable. Heavy shields and armor are a margin for risk, not a reason to absorb the entire enemy crossbow exchange.

**Commit:** attack suitable ordinary or protected infantry with support. The company can initiate a real contest; it need not wait for an enemy rout. If the army needs cavalry reception rather than melee decision, keep spears in the relevant position.

**Sustain:** keep shields facing and avoid overextending the leading files. If the enemy yields, gain the useful ground. If fresh reserves make the exchange unprofitable, consider relief rather than chasing prestige.

**Respond to crisis:** counterattack before a local collapse becomes a wing-wide rout. The company can buy time and turn a manageable contest, but it cannot guarantee recovery of an already lost flank.

**Finish:** secure the objective or reform for another task. Leave open pursuit to suitable mounted troops. A good result includes surviving company quality and a position the rest of the army can use.

## 25. Finite Hiring and Source Envelope

Use the ordinary independent mercenary recruitment list. A ready eligible offer shows strength, experience, price, upkeep, and replacement access. No domestic foot-knight building or bespoke captain negotiation is required.

The proposed envelope is route-linked, town, castle, and campaign gathering markets capable of assembling compatible well-equipped paid foot. Exact places and first eligible dates remain unset. Wealth alone does not create a company.

Availability is scarce but practical. A major market may support more than one offer if the actual pool design allows it. Do not impose a mythical Theater-wide one-company cap merely to signal prestige.

Funds, army capacity, access, and a ready company govern hiring. Research and decisions can unlock eligibility; they cannot create people, armor, shields, or available offers. Population informs finite pools without becoming a direct population-as-army-headcount subtraction model.

## 26. Cost, Retention, and Campaign Value

The proposed signing cost and upkeep are premium. The player buys immediate heavy-infantry access, not the cheapest long-term force.

| Comparison | Proposed relationship |
|---|---|
| Ordinary hired spears | Much greater expense and recovery burden |
| Hired Shield-and-Axe Men | Higher cost for protection, quality, and hard melee |
| Saxon Axe Veterans | Same broad premium decision space; exact ordering remains calibration-dependent |
| Comparable domestic heavy foot | Mercenary premium for external ready access |
| Mounted knights | No assumed universal price ordering; horses and role differ |

Retention can be worthwhile when experience and scarce access matter. Release can be sensible when domestic alternatives arrive or the treasury needs relief. Neither automatic peacetime dismissal nor permanent retention is required.

Do not balance through upkeep alone if immediate hire-dismiss cycles avoid the intended scarcity. Signing price, finite pools, recovery, and combat risk should work together. Keep the player interface simple.

## 27. Contract Persistence and Recovery

The employer buys service, not permanent ownership. Renewal, release, arrears, and willingness belong to the shared mercenary system. No random mid-battle betrayal or hidden mercenary cowardice modifier is proposed.

Dismissal preserves actual survivors, wounds, experience, leaders, and equipment condition. Rehire after genuine recovery may preserve company history; immediate dismissal cannot refill the card.

Reconstitution restores fit personnel, leadership, maces, shields, protection, morale, and collective timing at potentially different rates. A company can regain numbers before it regains the same quality.

Captured equipment can help where it is actually controlled and compatible. It does not create trained replacements. Gold cannot bypass an empty pool, and victory cannot reset casualties or repair every piece of kit.

A circulating company remains one company across hiring envelopes. Similar unit roles are acceptable; the same people and equipment cannot be sold twice under different aliases.

## 28. Supply, Baggage, Wounds, and Captivity

The company needs food, water, shelter, clothing, footwear, pay, repair support, and care for wounded personnel. These are shared sustainment requirements, not a special heavy-infantry logistics minigame.

Armor and shield maintenance make equipment readiness meaningful. A company can remain alive while losing part of the protection that justified its price. Aggregate readiness and recovery can represent that without individual equipment clicks.

Camp support includes repair materials, records, spare clothing, and wounded transport. Battlefield models are not the entire supported population. Baggage loss can harm recovery and morale.

Wounds, captives, missing personnel, ransom, exchange, and dispersal follow actual campaign outcomes. A routed battle card is not automatically a permanently destroyed company. No exact medical capability, ransom price, or recovery duration is invented.

## 29. Experience, Dismounting, and Era Persistence

An established company is the useful baseline. Veteran experience improves entry, guard recovery, facing, pursuit control, and reform. Heavy replacements may reduce those advantages without requiring a new unit family for every experience band.

This is a foot-service hire, not a free conversion from mounted knights. A future shared dismount system must preserve the actual company's people, horses, equipment, and service state rather than duplicating it into two army cards.

Equipment development can improve the family where chronology and supply support it. A later shieldless harness posture would need its own review; it is not silently substituted while retaining the active-shield benefits of this baseline.

Historical time advances +21 months per strategic turn, biological time +7 months, and campaign season one ordered Winter/Spring/Summer/Autumn step. Availability, aging, and recovery must not collapse those clocks into one.

Existing companies do not vanish because a date passes. New offers can change as sources and equipment evolve. No automatic conversion into pikes, halberdiers, or later sword specialists is assigned.

## 30. Campaign AI: Buy the Missing Capability

Recruit when the army needs a heavy melee decision or a durable counterattack reserve, has enough reach and support elsewhere, and can sustain the wages.

Avoid buying the card merely because it has a high defense value. If the army lacks cheap frontage, missile support, or cavalry reception, another purchase may solve the real problem more efficiently.

Retain useful experienced companies when replacement would lose scarce capability. Consider release when the role becomes redundant or the treasury cannot support it.

Finite offers, price, recovery, and role saturation constrain spam. Difficulty should improve decisions, not create unlimited companies or free armor outside the recruitment system.

## 31. Battle AI: Commit and Keep the Fight Useful

The AI must commit to suitable imperfect fights. Waiting forever for a perfect rear attack would waste a company designed to handle a hard infantry contest.

Target selection should consider reach, protection, facing, local strength, fatigue, missiles, cavalry, support, objective value, and exit space. The AI should prefer commitment when the target is ordinary or protected infantry, the approach is short or shieldable, at least one shoulder is secure, and the company can either hold the objective or receive relief. The AI should strongly avoid a deep prepared pike front, an open crossfire lane, or a fresh cavalry threat to the rear. These are desired behavior inputs, not implemented scoring claims.

Continue a productive melee rather than cycling charges for no reason. Reconsider when fresh enemy reserves, crossfire, cavalry, or congestion make the exchange deteriorate. A valuable objective can justify a costly hold, but the AI should understand that it is spending the company.

No perfect knowledge, instant turns, free disengagement, or hidden stamina is granted. The baseline card remains useful through ordinary adjacent attacks and direct supported commitments even if elaborate relief behavior is unavailable.

## 32. Autoresolve and Two-Way State

Autoresolve should preserve heavy protected melee value without turning the card into a universal high-defense token. Prepared reach, cavalry, missiles, fatigue, and support remain relevant.

A short guarded approach and a prolonged crossfire exposure should not resolve identically. Neither should a fresh ordinary spear front and an exhausted formation already turning away.

Campaign-to-battle coverage includes achieved strength, experience, leaders, equipment, morale, cohesion, fatigue, and supply. Battle-to-campaign coverage includes casualties, wounds, missing personnel, captives, leadership outcomes, equipment losses, experience, and recovery needs where shared systems support them.

These are proposed coverage requirements, not a verified runtime schema. No manual battle, autoresolve comparison, compile, or runtime test was run.

## 33. Player UI and Final Card Language

**Class:** Heavy Mace-and-Shield Infantry.

**Role:** Premium hired foot for hard infantry attacks, counterattacks, and contested objectives.

**Strengths:** Heavy protection, active shields, steady close fighting, useful pressure against well-equipped infantry.

**Weaknesses:** Short reach, high wages, scarce recovery, fatigue, flanking, and fresh cavalry impact.

**Use:** Reach a worthwhile fight in order, keep the shields facing, and turn the contest into ground the army can hold.

**Avoid:** Long exposed advances, deep prepared pikes, isolated pursuit, and fighting several fresh formations without relief.

**Short tooltip:** Heavily equipped hired foot with maces and shields. Built to win difficult infantry contests, but costly, short-ranged, and vulnerable when isolated.

**Extended tooltip:** German Foot Knights combine active shields, heavy protection, and repeated mace attacks. Use them to defeat well-equipped infantry or secure a contested objective. Protect their flanks and do not waste them against prepared pikes or fresh cavalry.

Campaign UI leads with price, upkeep, strength, experience, availability, and replacement access. Battle warnings should name the actual problem: exposed flank, blocked passage, fatigue, lost shields, or enemy cavalry. Text, animation, icons, and audio should reinforce one another; color alone is insufficient.

## 34. Visual Identity, Animation, and Audio

The distance read is heavy protected foot with maintained shields and compact maces. The silhouette must differ from the long hafts and two-handed posture of Saxon Axe Veterans.

Use dated equipment bands with individual variation rather than identical national uniforms. A restrained company marker is acceptable; royal or religious-order insignia is not implied. Main-body models must carry the protection promised by the card.

Avoid fantasy warhammers, chain flails as the baseline, oversized mace heads, backdated complete harness, and sword-dominant animations that erase the weapon identity.

Animation priorities are guarded approach, one-handed strike and recovery, shield facing, contact, short advance, turning, gap closure, fatigue, equipment loss, reform, withdrawal, surrender, and rout. Attack and shield poses must remain compatible.

Audio should sound like a working heavy company: shields forward, close up, hold, press, flank, halt, back together. These are English design prompts, not claimed historical commands. Card art should show a formed company at a contested infantry front, not a lone invulnerable champion. No image asset is generated here.

## 35. Flavor and Optional Company Events

Generated company labels can refer to a captain, route, marker, or remembered battle. They are fictional campaign flavor, not historical regiments.

Optional event ideas include a well-equipped company offering service, a competing employer, a repair burden after a hard siege, or a reputation earned by holding a decisive objective. They do not require a separate mini-faction mission chain.

Rewards should recognize actual results: win a contested sector, preserve a threatened formation, or withdraw in order after an expensive task. Avoid kill quotas that encourage wasteful pursuit.

Reputation can affect supported hiring terms. It does not create free fighters, free armor, or supernatural combat power.
