## 1. Strong Unit Verdict: Hew the Line

**Saxon Axe Veterans are hard-fighting, mail-equipped hired infantry whose two-handed axes give an army the power to defeat a selected infantry sector, not merely hold it.** They are the heavy offensive purchase above ordinary shield-and-axe men: fewer easy hires, higher wages, greater close-combat danger, and much less forgiveness under missiles or cavalry.

The signature is **Hew the Line**. The company closes in order, delivers a forceful entry, and keeps fighting through repeated two-handed actions while its frontage remains workable. It can attack ordinary shield infantry, punish a spear formation that has lost its clean reception, and counterattack enemy foot before a friendly sector collapses. It does not require an enemy already routing, a perfect rear charge, or a scripted handoff to earn its place. The signature produces four readable outcomes: **Clean Hew** when the enemy yields and the company remains connected; **Costly Hew** when useful ground is won through meaningful losses and fatigue; **Held Axe Fight** when neither front gives way; and **Spent Edge** when exposure, congestion, or exhaustion removes the company's offensive advantage.

These are veterans, not glass ornaments. Mail, experience, and purposeful close fighting let them remain in a suitable melee after the first exchange. Their weakness is **exposed staying power**, not an arbitrary timer that makes every axe ineffective after a charge. Against better-protected household infantry, concentrated missiles, cavalry, or a deep fresh spear front, that exposure becomes expensive.

The positive purchase is straightforward: **pay for a company that can win the hard infantry fight your ordinary line cannot finish.** The army still needs spears to secure its shoulders, missiles to shape the approach, and cavalry to exploit a larger collapse. The veterans provide the cutting edge, not the entire army.

## 3. Encyclopedia and Recruitment Text

An army may have enough men to hold a line and still lack the strength to break the enemy opposite it. Saxon Axe Veterans are hired for that difference. Their mail and helmets mark them as serious fighting men; their two-handed axes make their purpose unmistakable. They enter a selected infantry fight to win it, not to stand behind shields while the battle is decided elsewhere.

A company kept together can strike hard on entry and continue with measured, repeated blows. Ordinary shielded foot must contend with a dangerous opponent at close range, while spearmen who turn, lose alignment, or become entangled can find their reach advantage slipping away. The veterans are particularly useful beside a steady spear line, where another formation secures the front and the axemen can attack the enemy's exposed shoulder.

The same weapons leave their users without active shields in the main fighting posture. Missile fire can spoil the approach, a fresh mounted charge can overwhelm an exposed wing, and a prolonged contest against superior heavy infantry can consume the company without producing a decision. A commander who spends these troops on every minor skirmish soon pays veteran wages for a diminished formation.

Their service is bought through the mercenary market. Availability is finite, replacements are not guaranteed, and restoring experienced files takes more than issuing new axes. A surviving company may be worth retaining through several campaigns, provided its wages buy a role the army still needs.

**Recruitment text:** Veteran hired axemen with mail and powerful two-handed weapons. Strong in infantry attacks and counterattacks; exposed to missiles, cavalry, and attacks on their flanks.

This is authored game encyclopedia copy. It does not claim that an attested historical regiment bore this English display name.

## 8. Two-Handed Weapon Contract and Contact Resolution

The primary attack uses both hands. Reach, preparation, contact, follow-through, and recovery must remain coherent with that posture. The fighter cannot simultaneously deliver the heavy action and receive a full active-shield defense value.

The intended advantage is a dangerous combination of reach within close infantry combat, forceful committed attacks, and veteran timing. This can be expressed through ordinary attack, damage, contact, defense, and fatigue tuning. It does not require individual physics for every axe head.

An enemy shield can block, deflect, bind, or be displaced where the shared model supports those outcomes. An axe contact is not automatic shield destruction. Substantial armor remains meaningful; any protected-target advantage must be calibrated rather than treating all protection as absent.

A compact cut or controlled recovery can be useful in moderate congestion. Extreme crowding still restricts the weapon. Do not make the unit helpless whenever a tree is nearby, but do not let full-width swings pass through friendly bodies, walls, or occupied ranks.

## 9. Hew the Line: The Full Combat Loop

**Prepare:** select a target the army can actually reach and support. Face the company, keep a usable frontage, and avoid spending its freshness on a needless long run.

**Enter:** use an ordered advance or short charge according to distance and pressure. A favorable entry creates immediate danger, but the unit must also function when already close or when counterattacking from a halt.

**Fight:** maintain repeated heavy-axe work. The company can win a real ordinary infantry contest through sustained pressure, not just a single charge statistic. Enemy defense, reach, armor, support, and morale continue to matter.

**Read:** watch whether the enemy is yielding, whether the leading files remain connected, and whether a reserve or cavalry threat is changing the bargain. Ground gained is useful only if the company can survive there.

**Exploit or hold:** advance into actual space, hold a won sector, or turn a manageable shoulder. A local success need not produce an army-wide rout to be valuable.

**Recover or disengage:** halt pursuit, accept relief where possible, or withdraw under support. Halting restores order only to the extent that time and space permit; it does not heal wounds or reset fatigue.

This loop uses ordinary orders. Hew the Line is the unit's readable identity, not a compulsory activated stance, cooldown, or multi-button ritual.

## 11. Charge, Entry, and Sustained Offense

The first contact should feel stronger than the medium shield-and-axe company's entry. That difference comes from the weapon posture, veteran commitment, and attack tuning, not a scripted knockback wave.

Charge value must remain distinct from sustained melee value. A company that reaches the fight without a running start can still deliver dangerous attacks. Conversely, repeatedly withdrawing and charging should not become the only efficient use if the unit was bought for heavy infantry combat.

A failed charge is not automatically a lost battle. The veterans may settle into a hard contest if their order and condition remain usable. The player then weighs the value of continuing against the risk of losing an expensive cadre.

The unit should not be designed as a disposable impact missile. Its mail and veteran handling buy enough staying power to complete a suitable fight. That does not mean it should grind indefinitely against superior heavy troops or replace a shielded holding line under fire.

## 11A. Decisive Separation from Shield-and-Axe Men

The mature distinction is now explicit:

- **Approach:** shield-and-axe men are safer against directional missile fire; Saxon Axe Veterans need better screening or a shorter exposed route.
- **Entry:** Saxon Axe Veterans create the stronger first close-combat threat when delivered in usable order.
- **Sustained fight:** both units can remain useful, but the veterans produce more offensive pressure while the shielded company preserves defense more economically.
- **Attrition:** the veterans lose more campaign value per casualty because their mailed and experienced core is harder to restore.
- **Line duty:** shield-and-axe men remain the better practical choice for repeated ordinary holding and relief tasks.
- **Decision point:** the veterans are bought to turn a contested infantry sector; the shielded company is bought to fight more safely and flexibly across several ordinary sectors.

If testing shows that the veterans approach as safely, hold as cheaply, and receive cavalry as well as the shielded or spear companies, the veteran card is overtuned. If testing shows they cannot beat suitable ordinary infantry without a rear attack or enemy rout, the card is undertuned.

No shield wall, spear brace, pike block, or instantaneous emergency ring is granted. The player still controls one unit card; internal depth does not create independently commanded reserve companies.

Understrength formations must shorten their front, reduce depth, or accept gaps. Experience cannot fill missing ground. Rear ranks may support and replace losses, but cannot deliver full attacks through occupied friendly bodies.

## 13. Leadership and Veteran Company Character

The captain and subordinate leaders keep the attack coherent: choose the point, dress the frontage, signal commitment, halt pursuit, face a new threat, and restore usable order. They are not aura generators.

Veteran value should appear in steadier entry, better response to ordinary pressure, less wasteful pursuit, and more reliable recovery than inexperienced foot. The baseline already contains that value; further campaign experience improves it without unlocking the unit's basic ability to fight.

Leader loss can slow orders, weaken rallying, and leave parts of the formation reacting unevenly. It should not force an automatic rout on a healthy company. Equally, a surviving captain cannot make an encircled and exhausted remnant behave as if nothing has happened.

The company can acquire a generated history through service. Its reputation should arise from actual outcomes, not a compulsory famous captain or fabricated historical pedigree.

## 14. Morale, Cohesion, and Fatigue

Morale is steady rather than unbreakable. A company with a clear task, nearby support, usable leaders, and a route out should be willing to accept a serious infantry fight. It should not rout merely because it is mercenary.

Cohesion measures whether the company can continue acting together. Winning individual exchanges while losing both shoulders can leave the veterans in a worse tactical position. A visible local success and an overall dangerous commitment can coexist.

Fatigue comes from movement, terrain, equipment burden, repeated attacks, defensive work, and forced turns. It should reduce attack rhythm and recovery progressively rather than switching the company off after a fixed assault window.

A pause can restore some condition where the shared model permits. It cannot replace casualties, repair mail, or recover a lost captain. Repeatedly shifting the reserve across the field has a real cost even if it has not yet fought.

## 15. Missile Interaction and the Approach

The missing active shield is the clearest price of the heavy-axe posture. Mail provides protection according to the shared armor model, but the company should be a less comfortable missile target than shielded troops only where actual equipment and circumstances justify it, never by a hidden veteran immunity.

The intended comparison is simple: shield-and-axe men are safer during a comparable exposed frontal approach; Saxon Axe Veterans are more dangerous after successful delivery. Crossfire, close javelins, and strong crossbows should make unsupported commitment costly.

The answer is ordinary combined arms: terrain, screening, suppression, a shorter exposed route, or accepting measured losses for a valuable contact. Screening is not a magical projectile barrier. Friendly missiles must cease or shift when the melee masks their lane.

Loose approach is an option if the shared formation system supports it. Reforming before contact takes time and space. The AI and player should see the tradeoff rather than receiving both dispersed missile resistance and perfect dense melee at once.

## 16. Cavalry Interaction and Mounted Threat

A long axe is not a formation spear. The unit has no prepared spear brace and no universal anti-cavalry multiplier. Fresh cavalry with room to charge remains a severe threat, particularly against a flank, rear, or exhausted formation.

The veterans can be dangerous to riders who are already stalled in close combat or trapped by terrain and other troops. That emergency capability should not be erased merely to enforce a simplistic counter triangle. It also must not make the company a safer cavalry receiver than spears.

Friendly spears, obstacles, and mounted support protect the approach and the return lane. Cavalry can influence the veterans without charging: a threat to the exit may force the player to halt an otherwise successful infantry attack.

Pursuit belongs primarily to suitable mounted troops. The axemen may take a short local opportunity, but chasing riders across open ground is a poor use of veteran infantry.

## 17. Friendly Passage, Relief, and Counterattack

The best reserve attack is often beside a fixed friendly front rather than through it. A real interval, a clear shoulder, or a deliberate withdrawal can admit the veterans. Occupied friendly models cannot be treated as transparent.

A counterattack can also reinforce a bounded gap before the line fully collapses. The company does not require a pristine parade-ground handoff. Some files may enter while others are delayed; that imperfect result should remain playable without becoming free passage.

Relief is valuable after a hard fight, but it is not guaranteed. A pinned company may be unable to disengage cleanly. Friendly congestion, cavalry, lost signals, or enemy pursuit can turn an attempted withdrawal into additional losses.

The player should be rewarded for leaving return space. This is ordinary positional judgment, not a unique axe-company minigame or a mandatory formation puzzle.

## 18. Defensive and Emergency Employment

The company can defend itself and hold a limited sector. Veteran heavy infantry should not become useless because the enemy initiates contact. A counterblow against ordinary attackers is part of the positive design.

It is less efficient as a prolonged missile-facing anvil or a broad anti-cavalry front. Guarding a narrow camp entrance can reduce exposure, but a choke can also restrict axe work and prevent relief.

Emergency commitment may be worthwhile even when conditions are imperfect. Saving a missile battery, preventing a local breakthrough, or buying time for a retreat can justify expensive losses. The profile must distinguish a costly useful sacrifice from a nonsensical attack.

No automatic line-restoration power is assigned. If the whole wing is routing under cavalry, one veteran company may be unable to reverse it.

## 20. Siege, Camp, and Garrison Portfolio

The strongest siege use is a selected infantry assault where the company can deploy: a secured gate, wide breach exit, courtyard, or contested street. The veterans can be a dangerous second wave, but they may also lead a manageable entry if the tactical conditions justify it.

They are not automatically ideal ladder troops. A narrow stair, missile-swept wall, or rubble choke can deny the weapon and consume the company before its quality matters. The design should not label every axe unit a breach specialist regardless of geometry. Their preferred siege assault is **post-access violence**: another element secures the physical entry, then the veterans widen the foothold, defeat the defending infantry reserve, and hold enough space for follow-on troops.

Camp roles include counterattacking a penetration, supporting a sortie, and guarding a threatened inner approach. These are ordinary heavy-infantry tasks, not engineering powers. Axes do not grant wall demolition or automatic gate destruction.

Garrison service continues to cost wages unless the shared economy explicitly provides otherwise. Embarkation requires actual transport and landing recovery. This profile does not create sailors, marines, or a naval mercenary card.

## 21. Whole-Battle Employment: Germania

**Deployment:** place the veterans behind or beside a sector where infantry decision is likely. Protect the outer shoulder and preserve a route forward. Do not put them in the first missile-catching rank merely because they wear mail.

**Opening:** let Woodland Hunters pressure exposed targets and German Crossbowmen contest suitable protected ones where those neighboring designs permit. The veterans can advance with the army rather than remaining frozen in reserve until a perfect flank appears.

**Commitment:** attack an ordinary infantry sector that can be reached with support. A spear company can fix the enemy while the veterans enter beside it; shield-and-axe men can hold a neighboring fight without being expected to absorb every casualty for them.

**Decision:** use the company's superior offensive threat to force a local result. If the opponent yields, gain the useful ground. If a fresh heavy reserve arrives, decide whether the objective is worth the escalating cost.

**Crisis:** a counterattack before collapse may save a sector. A late attack into an already lost wing may simply add another expensive rout. Veteran quality improves the chance of a useful intervention, not certainty.

**After success:** halt deep pursuit, secure the sector, accept relief, or re-form for another task. Mounted troops exploit suitable open pursuit. A successful battle ends with the company still useful, not merely with an impressive kill total.

## 23. Pursuit, Withdrawal, Rout, and Captivity

A short pursuit can complete a local success. A long pursuit can expose the company to fresh reserves, missiles, or cavalry. Veteran discipline should make a halt more reliable, not instantaneous.

Withdrawal requires a direction, signals, space, and a manageable enemy. Relief or terrain may help. The unit cannot freely cancel a hard melee and appear behind the line without losses.

Rout can follow encirclement, leader loss, heavy casualties, exhaustion, or army collapse. A routed battle card is not automatically a permanently destroyed company; survivors, missing personnel, captives, and equipment must return through the shared campaign outcome rules.

Captivity, ransom, exchange, and dispersal remain conditional on actual results. A captured captain can be particularly damaging to reconstitution, but ordinary fighters are not automatically valueless or guaranteed to return.

## 24. Hiring Interface and Finite Availability

Use the ordinary independent mercenary list. An eligible ready offer shows strength, experience, hire price, upkeep, and replacement access. Hiring does not require a domestic axe building, a bespoke captain negotiation, or a mini-faction diplomacy screen.

The proposed source envelope is northern Germania-facing markets, pending historical and geographic review. It is not a list of activated towns. A wealthy province does not automatically contain a ready veteran company.

Availability should be scarcer than general hired foot but sufficient to support a real player choice. Do not reduce the unit to a mythical one-off merely to communicate prestige. Multiple companies can exist if the actual pool design supports them.

Funds, army capacity, access, and an available company govern the hire. Gold cannot bypass an empty pool. Research or decisions can unlock eligibility but cannot create trained people, equipment, or ready companies.

## 25. Relative Economy and the Retention Decision

The intended signing cost and upkeep are substantially above ordinary hired foot. Within the Early Era infantry portfolio, the veterans are a premium offensive purchase, not necessarily the most expensive military card of every class.

| Comparison | Proposed economic direction |
|---|---|
| Domestic ordinary infantry | Mercenary veterans cost more for ready specialist access |
| Hired Spear Companies | Higher price and more demanding replacement burden |
| Hired Shield-and-Axe Men | Higher price for stronger close offense, not better all-purpose safety |
| Household-equivalent hires | Do not assume the veterans exceed every elite cost |
| Repeated emergency hiring | Finite pools and signing costs prevent effortless replacement spam |

Retention should be a real decision. Keeping an experienced company preserves a valuable fighting tool, but wages continue during quiet periods. Releasing it can be sensible when domestic capability arrives or the treasury needs relief.

Do not solve balance through upkeep alone if instant hire-dismiss cycles avoid the intended cost. Signing price, scarcity, recovery, and battlefield risk should work together without adding unnecessary contract paperwork.

## 26. Contract, Dismissal, and Cross-Theater Circulation

The employer buys service, not permanent ownership. Renewal, release, arrears, and future access belong to the shared mercenary system.

Dismissal preserves actual survivors, wounds, experience, leadership, equipment condition, and recovery needs. It must not refill the company or restore a dead veteran core. Rehire after genuine recovery can preserve history.

A company moving between hiring envelopes remains one company. The same offer cannot appear simultaneously under a Saxon label in Germania and a Norse or generic label elsewhere. This is ordinary pool accounting, not a demand for exhaustive relationship authorities before the unit can be designed.

No random mid-battle betrayal, national loyalty multiplier, or hidden mercenary cowardice penalty is proposed. Payment trouble can affect willingness and access through supported campaign rules rather than unexplained combat collapse.

## 27. Replenishment and Veteran Reconstitution

Restore the company through compatible personnel, leaders, weapons, protection, care, and time. Headcount and quality need not recover at the same rate.

A few replacements can integrate into a surviving veteran core. Heavy replacement may restore numbers while reducing shared timing and experience. This can remain a condition of the same card; it does not require a new low-grade unit family for every casualty band.

Recovered axes and mail can help repair stocks. They do not create veteran fighters. Captured equipment is useful only where the army actually controls and can use it.

The practical player read is strength, experience, readiness, and replacement access. Do not make the player inspect individual mail links or approve every haft repair.

Reject hire-dismiss-rehire refill, victory-screen full restoration, gold-created veterans, and the same people counted simultaneously in domestic and mercenary service. Allow real recovery where compatible capacity exists.

## 28. Supply, March, Baggage, and Medical Recovery

The company needs food, water, shelter, footwear, clothing, pay, weapon maintenance, protection repair, and care for wounded personnel. These are shared sustainment requirements, not a bespoke axe logistics simulator.

Operational mobility is that of equipped foot. Forced marches can deliver the company sooner at the cost of fatigue and wear. The card receives no hidden strategic speed because its members are veterans.

Camp support includes repair materials, spare clothing, wounded transport, and company records. Battlefield models are not the entire supported population. Losing baggage can damage readiness and recovery without requiring every support person to appear as a combat model.

Wounds may heal, reduce capability, or remove personnel from service according to the shared medical model. No exact treatment capability or recovery duration is invented here. Replacing headcount does not erase the consequences of wounded veterans or lost leaders.

## 29. Equipment Lifecycle and Depleted States

Axe loss reduces the core offensive proposition. Mail loss or damage reduces protection. Helmet loss, damaged hafts, and depleted sidearms can affect readiness where the shared model represents them.

The profile does not require persistent individual equipment damage to be implemented before the card can exist. If the battle system lacks that detail, aggregate equipment grade and recovery can represent the practical consequences. Do not claim a subsystem is running merely because this document names it.

Depleted art should retain the unit's identity: repaired kit, missing equipment, reduced density, and visible campaign wear. It should not turn a serious veteran company into a comic ragged mob.

A fallback sidearm is not an invisible replacement axe. A company that has lost its main weapons can survive as personnel while no longer delivering the original card's full battlefield value.

## 30. Experience and Era Development

The first hire is already veteran-quality by design. Additional experience improves judgment, cohesion, and recovery; it does not unlock basic competence.

Early Era is the intended design band, not certified January 1062 availability. Later continuation may reflect surviving companies, compatible recruitment, and maintained equipment. The calendar alone should not delete an existing formation.

No automatic 1160 retirement is adopted from Stainless Steel. No automatic transition to German Foot Knights, halberdiers, or Doppelsoldner is proposed. Better mail or more consistent helmets can be development within the same family rather than a new card.

The three clocks remain distinct: historical progression is +21 months per strategic turn; biological progression is +7 months; campaign season advances one ordered Winter/Spring/Summer/Autumn step. Availability, aging, and recovery must not silently use one clock as a substitute for the others.

## 31. Campaign AI: Recruit, Retain, and Release

Recruit when the army needs infantry decision, has enough frontage and support to deliver the company, and can afford the continuing cost. A spear-heavy army facing ordinary foot is a strong proposed use case.

Avoid recruiting simply because the card has a high attack value. An army already saturated with assault infantry may need spears, missiles, or cavalry instead. A treasury unable to sustain the contract should not buy prestige and immediately collapse.

Retain a useful veteran company when replacing it would lose valuable experience or access. Consider release when the role becomes redundant, domestic alternatives arrive, or wages outweigh expected use.

Finite pools, recovery, price, and role saturation should constrain spam. Difficulty should improve purchasing decisions rather than create unlimited veteran companies outside the recruitment system.

## 32. Battle AI: Commitment Without Paralysis

The AI must be willing to use the unit. It should not wait indefinitely for an enemy already routing or a perfect rear attack. A supported attack on suitable ordinary infantry is a valid commitment.

Target selection should consider enemy reach, protection, facing, condition, local support, missile exposure, cavalry, approach distance, and return space. These are desired inputs, not a claim of existing AI code.

Once committed, the AI should continue a productive fight rather than repeatedly charge-cycling for no reason. It should also recognize a stalled costly attack, a fresh heavy reserve, or a cavalry threat to the exit. The AI should prefer commitment where at least two of the following are true: the target is ordinary rather than elite heavy foot; the target is already fixed or turning; the approach is short or screened; the veterans have a protected shoulder; and a usable halt or withdrawal lane exists. This is a qualitative behavior target, not a claim of implemented scoring.

Do not grant perfect enemy knowledge, instant turns, free disengagement, or hidden stamina. If the shared AI cannot execute a complex relief, the baseline card must remain useful through ordinary adjacent attacks and manageable direct commitments.

## 33. Autoresolve and Persistent Outcomes

Autoresolve should represent the company's strong infantry offense and the cost of delivering it. A generic heavy-infantry score must not erase missile exposure or make it a universal cavalry counter.

Relevant proposed inputs include effective strength, experience, leadership, equipment, fatigue, terrain, enemy reach and protection, missile pressure, cavalry, and friendly support. A supported attack and an exposed approach should not resolve identically.

Losses return to the campaign as personnel, wounds, captives, leadership outcomes, equipment condition, experience, cohesion, and recovery need where the shared systems support them. No victory reset is permitted.

The unit should not receive a flat anti-shield or anti-armor score that ignores the actual opponent. Qualitative targets require later comparison with manual battles; no numerical result is claimed.

## 34. Player UI, Accessibility, and Order Simplicity

The campaign card leads with price, upkeep, strength, experience, availability, and replacement access. The battle card leads with heavy two-handed axe infantry, strong infantry offense, no active shield, and cavalry or missile danger.

Useful state messages include approaching, engaged, gaining ground, stalled, exposed flank, fatigued, reforming, withdrawing, and routing. The warning should name the practical problem rather than merely flash a generic danger icon.

Use text, silhouette, animation, and audio together. Color alone must not carry the distinction between a healthy attack and an overextended one.

Normal move, attack, halt, guard, and withdraw orders cover the core. Hew the Line is not another button the player must remember. Detailed company accounting stays behind readable strength and readiness summaries.

## 34A. Final Player-Facing Card Language

**Class:** Veteran Heavy Axe Infantry

**Role:** Premium mercenary assault infantry for defeating ordinary shielded foot and counterattacking committed enemy infantry.

**Strengths:** Powerful two-handed attacks, strong veteran cohesion, mail-centered protection, sustained offensive value after entry.

**Weaknesses:** No active shield, vulnerable approach, exposed flanks, poor cavalry reception, expensive casualties and recovery.

**Use:** Deliver beside a steady front, win the selected infantry contest, then halt before the company becomes isolated or exhausted.

**Avoid:** Long exposed advances, fresh spear points, formed cavalry, crossfire, and prolonged unsupported combat against superior heavy infantry.

**Short tooltip:** Mailed veteran axemen hired to break a selected infantry sector. Dangerous after contact, but costly and exposed without shields.

**Flavor line:** Mail for the return blow. Two hands for the answer.

## 35. Visual Identity and Model Portfolio

At battle distance the read is **mail, helmets, long axe hafts, and two-handed fighting posture**. This must be visibly heavier and more committed than the medium shield-and-axe company without drifting into later plate infantry.

Use a coherent mix of maintained mail, practical garments, dated helmet candidates, and serviceable axes. The captain and veteran leaders can have better-finished kit, but the main body must still support the heavy-infantry promise.

Avoid horned helmets, mandatory fur costumes, oversized double-headed axes, identical royal livery, and ornamental fantasy armor. “Saxon” is not a license for a costume stereotype.

The art brief can propose a restrained company marker and varied personal equipment. Exact motifs, garment cuts, helmet forms, and axe geometry require dated review. No image asset is generated in this task.

## 36. Animation, Audio, and Card Art Direction

Animation priorities are ordered approach, two-handed ready posture, forceful entry, repeated controlled attacks, recovery, local defense, turning, gap closure, fatigue, weapon loss, reform, withdrawal, surrender, and rout.

The recovery matters as much as the strike. Avoid endless windmill swings, attacks through friendly ranks, or full active-shield defense during two-handed actions. A compact action can fit a tighter lane, but no animation should imply that walls and bodies are irrelevant.

Audio should communicate a working veteran company: close up, axes ready, forward, hold, keep the flank, halt, back together. These are English design prompts, not claimed historical commands. Constant berserker roaring would misrepresent the intended discipline.

Card art should show the heavy axe and mail clearly, with a formed company behind the foreground fighter. The image should sell a dangerous hired infantry force, not a lone mythic champion.

## 37. Flavor and Optional Company Events

Generated company names may refer to a captain, a road, a marker, or a remembered battle. They must be identified as fictional campaign flavor, not historical regiments.

Useful event concepts include a veteran body offering service, a competing employer, a company seeking time to restore its wounded core, or a reputation gained after a decisive counterattack. These are optional shared-system ideas, not implemented events or guaranteed scripts.

Rewards should recognize meaningful outcomes: win a contested sector, save a threatened formation, or withdraw the company in order after a hard task. Avoid kill quotas that encourage wasteful pursuit.

Reputation can influence hiring terms where supported. It does not create free replacements, grant supernatural combat power, or require a separate faction-style mission chain.

## 38. Historical Evidence and Mod Adaptation

The opened Stainless Steel **Saxon Huscarls** page describes English mercenaries with two-handed axes and mail. It lists England availability until 1180 and northern Germany until 1160. Those are the mod's statements and availability choices, not independent proof of a continental Saxon mercenary corps.

The useful inheritance is the clear Total War proposition: superior mailed two-handed axe infantry hired at a premium. The rebuilt design adapts that proposition rather than copying its numerical statistics, national identity, dates, or blanket mercenary penalties.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art's **Ax Head**, object 2023.580.5, is catalogued as Scandinavian, eleventh–twelfth century, and a broad-axe form. It is narrow material-culture orientation only. It does not establish a Germania company, exact haft, fighting drill, equipment distribution, or opening availability. Its decorated status should not become the standard kit of every model.
