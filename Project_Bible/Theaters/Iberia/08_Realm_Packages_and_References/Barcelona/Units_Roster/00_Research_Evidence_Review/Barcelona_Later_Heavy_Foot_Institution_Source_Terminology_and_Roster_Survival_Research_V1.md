## 3. General Men-at-Arms Context

A general reference defines a man-at-arms as a soldier of the High Medieval to Renaissance eras, usually serving as a fully armored heavy cavalryman, but states that such personnel could and in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries often did fight on foot. The same source says the term denotes a military function rather than a social rank and notes that not every man-at-arms was a knight. citeturn26search228

```yaml
man_at_arms_as_military_function: SupportedGenerally
man_at_arms_always_knight: false
man_at_arms_usually_mounted_heavy_function: SupportedGenerally
fourteenth_and_fifteenth_century_fighting_on_foot: SupportedGenerally
Barcelona_specific_source: none
```

This supports a potential dismounted expression of actual mounted or high-status personnel. It does not establish a permanent Barcelona foot-only institution.

## 4. Dismounting as an Assignment Decision

A scholarly chapter summary states that the choice to fight mounted or on foot depended on practical considerations and social, chivalric, national, and traditional norms. The summary lists mounted tasks such as scouting, flank and forager protection, reconnaissance in force, raids, fixing enemy infantry, and pursuit, while describing infantry as naturally defensive because of lower mobility. citeturn26search231

```yaml
dismounting_decision_is_contextual: SupportedGenerally
mounted_personnel_can_have_distinct_mounted_tasks: SupportedGenerally
foot_service_should_not_automatically_create_new_people: true
foot_expression_should_consume_actual_contributors_horses_and_duties: true
```

The best initial design treatment is assignment-based: an eligible mounted or high-status source may fight on foot when the mission, terrain, horse risk, command, equipment, and formation permit. Dismounting cannot duplicate personnel or leave horses and handlers cost-free.

## 5. General Chronology Firewall

A modern secondary article says dismounted men-at-arms appear most clearly from the late thirteenth century onward and are prominent in fourteenth- and fifteenth-century warfare, particularly in northern France, the Low Countries, and the British Isles. Because this is a general article and not Barcelona evidence, the research uses it only to reinforce the late chronology and regional caution. citeturn26search227


The source's geographic emphasis also cautions against importing northern French or English heavy-foot doctrine into Barcelona without direct evidence.
