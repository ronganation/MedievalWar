## 2. Controlling Denmark Military Thesis

Denmark is a negotiated Christian maritime kingdom whose military coherence depends upon royal authority, assemblies, regional contribution, households, settlements, church institutions, properties, supply, land and sea routes, ports, crossings, ships, crews, command, and enforceable decisions.

```yaml
one_universal_levy_pool: false
```

## 4. Corrected Historical Development Thesis

The double-check rejects a simple statement that Denmark becomes feudal earlier than Norway. The safer and more useful design is uneven development through royal concentration, hird and household transformation, mounted retinues, landholding and tax privilege, fiscal substitution, towns, ports, church institutions, continental contact, and Baltic warfare.

The supplied Scandinavian material describes knighthood developing slowly, hird service persisting, levy and fleet obligations continuing, cavalry becoming more important by the later twelfth century, and the Danish hird changing or disappearing by the later twelfth century as a newer knightly class emerged. Those points support coexistence and transformation, not an automatic conversion from levy realm to feudal roster.

```yaml
safe_design_direction:
  - EarlyHirdAndRegionalContributionCoexist
  - MountedHouseholdServiceBeginsScarce
  - CavalryImportanceMayGrowThroughTwelfthAndThirteenthCenturyDevelopment
  - HirdFamiliesMayTransformMergePersistOrDecline
  - LevyAndRegionalObligationMayPersistBesideFiscalAndProfessionalForms
  - CashOrProvisionSubstitutionMayAlterServiceWithoutDeletingSourcePopulations
prohibited_shortcut:
  - DateCreatesKnights
  - FeudalizationCreatesHorsesArmorOrTraining
  - CentralizationCreatesReadyArmy
  - TaxPrivilegeCreatesMountedUnitAutomatically
```

## 5. Evidence-Backed Equipment and Service Envelope

Ian Heath's Scandinavian knight material describes thirteenth-century equipment comparable to Western European knightly equipment and notes Danish evidence among Scandinavian examples. The Scandinavian hirdman material describes heavy infantry equipment including gambesons, shields, swords, spears, mail for some contexts, bows, and continuing axe use. The Scandinavian militia material states that iron helmets were basic equipment in Denmark and Sweden by the twelfth century at the latest, while regulation arms could include shield, spear, sword or axe, with bows or crossbows and ammunition associated with rowing thwarts or individuals. citeturn36view193turn36view194turn36view195

```yaml
Early1062_caution:
  - DoNotBackProjectLateTwelfthOrThirteenthCenturyEquipmentUniformly
  - PreserveMixedEquipmentAndSourceVariation
  - TreatCrossbowsAsAResearchAndDistributionQuestionNotAutomaticMassIssue
  - TreatHeavyCavalryAsScarceAndSourceDependent
HighEra_direction:
  - BetterArmorAmongHouseholdRetainedAndMountedFamilies
  - BowAndCrossbowCoexistence
  - LongerHaftedAxesAndImprovedInfantryProtectionWhereSupported
  - KnightlyEquipmentConvergenceWherePeopleHorsesTrainingAndInstitutionsPass
```
