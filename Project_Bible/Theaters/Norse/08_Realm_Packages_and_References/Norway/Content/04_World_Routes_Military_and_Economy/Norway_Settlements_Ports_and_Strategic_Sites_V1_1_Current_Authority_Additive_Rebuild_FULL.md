## 1. Concise Verdict

Represent January 1062 Norway as a long maritime kingdom held together by coastal movement, fjords, regional centers, royal itinerancy, assemblies, ecclesiastical sites, markets, protected anchorages, landings, and inland approaches rather than by one permanent capital or a continuous modern road network.

Use a deliberately bounded core. Nidaros is the strongest opening political, ecclesiastical, assembly, river-mouth, and maritime-center candidate. Tønsberg, Oslo, Sarpsborg, and Borgund are important southern or western settlement candidates, but each requires field-specific archaeological, chronological, and Registry review before activation. Bergen must not be loaded as a fully established opening city merely because it becomes important soon afterward. Stavanger, Hamar, later fortresses, later bishoprics, later castles, and later urban institutions remain deferred or conditional unless January 1062 evidence independently supports the exact object.

The strategic geography must distinguish physical existence from political association, legal ownership, military control, access, port operation, route capacity, settlement status, institutional occupancy, and player knowledge.

## 5. Norway Strategic-Site Thesis

Norway's site network should create four strategic truths:

- distance matters because the kingdom is long and coastal;
- maritime access is essential but seasonal and capacity-dependent;
- royal and institutional authority depends on presence, communication, and regional cooperation;
- a small number of important centers cannot substitute for local landings, anchorages, assembly venues, farms, fisheries, timber, crossings, and inland approaches.

The map should not reduce Norway to isolated coastal cities. It should expose the relationship among settlements, fjords, valleys, assemblies, churches, royal residences, resource zones, and maritime corridors.
