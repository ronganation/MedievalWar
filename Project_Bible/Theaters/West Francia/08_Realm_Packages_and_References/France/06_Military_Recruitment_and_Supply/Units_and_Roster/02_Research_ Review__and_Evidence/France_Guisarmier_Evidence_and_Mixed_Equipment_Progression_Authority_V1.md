## 4. Verified Claims from the Armed-Peasant Page

The reviewed page explicitly states:

- figures of armed peasants appear in contemporary sources from the mid-twelfth century;
- lower-class infantry using everyday tools and makeshift weapons probably appeared in armies involving general levies earlier;
- the depicted figures come from manuscripts showing peasants in military rather than agricultural situations;
- the listed characteristic weapons include scythes, pitchforks, and flails;
- stakes, picks, clubs, clod-mallets, spades, and staff weapons also appear;
- bows, spears, and axes are described as minority equipment;
- the referenced troops are characterized as very low quality;
- clothing is described as largely unbleached wool or linen, with dull colors also appearing;
- arming caps are noted on the two mid-thirteenth-century figures.

## 5. Armed-Peasant Evidence Boundary

The page supports a low-status and low-coherence military environment. It does not establish:

- a France-specific unit family;
- a universal peasant weapon distribution;
- one organized Communal Guisarmier formation;
- one stable service institution;
- one exact period before the mid-twelfth-century visual examples;
- a movable field unit for every community;
- useful battlefield quality beyond the author’s very-low-quality characterization.

## 6. Verified Claims from the Staff-Weapon Page

The reviewed page explicitly states:

- some staff weapons were initially based on agricultural implements;
- those weapons developed into sophisticated infantry staff weapons;
- by the thirteenth century long-handled weapons were known by many names;
- listed names include godendac, croc, faus, fauchard, glaive, pikte, gisarme, voulge, bill, and halberd;
- terminology is often unclear because descriptions can be very similar;
- many forms appear to have shared an ancestry involving a long broad cutting blade with points or hooks;
- the gisarme appears in English and French sources from the twelfth century onward;
- Heath describes the gisarme as an axe type with an elongated crescent blade;
- the page identifies two gisarme examples from French sources of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries;
- Heath states that a gisarme with a rear spike appears to have been known as a voulge;
- Heath states that the term voulge appears in French sources from the twelfth century;
- the bill is described as appearing in the twelfth century and predominantly English;
- the fauchard is described as appearing mainly in French and occasionally English sources from the twelfth to late fourteenth centuries.

## 7. Staff-Weapon Evidence Boundary

The page does not establish:

- one universally accepted modern typology;
- one exact weapon corresponding to every medieval term;
- one universal battlefield function for all listed weapons;
- a standardized French Guisarmier corps;
- a standardized French Voulgier corps;
- direct lineage from every agricultural tool to a military weapon;
- exact production, recruitment, or unit organization;
- exact numerical prevalence.

- improvised emergency levy;
- organized spear infantry;
- broader Guisarmier disruption infantry;
- a later coherent Voulgier family if admitted.
