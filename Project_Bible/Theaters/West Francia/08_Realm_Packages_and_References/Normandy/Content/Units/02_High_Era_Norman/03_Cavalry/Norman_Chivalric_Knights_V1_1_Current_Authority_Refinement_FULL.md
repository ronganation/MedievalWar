## 5. One-Screen Player Card

- **Class:** Late-High Norman elite chivalric heavy cavalry
- **Source:** Qualified knightly retainers and supporting mounted personnel with warhorses, remounts, lances, swords, shields, armor, grooms, farriers, officers, forage, pay, quarters, and political service
- **Availability:** Extremely narrow, expensive, politically visible, and slow to recover
- **Battle promise:** Deliver a decisive charge into a disrupted, exposed, pinned, or poorly protected target, then disengage, reform, and threaten again
- **Best use:** Open firm ground, flank attacks, countercharges, reserve commitment, exploitation, pursuit of broken enemies, and combined-arms shock
- **Worst use:** Frontal impact against ordered spears, dense woodland, marsh, steep slopes, walls, streets, bridges, deep mud, exhausted horses, and prolonged melee
- **Strategic cost:** Concentrates elite retainers, expensive horses, remounts, armor, lances, grooms, farriers, horse transports, forage, prestige, patronage, and household military capacity
- **Counterplay:** Spears, depth, disciplined reserves, missiles, obstacles, rough or wet terrain, horse fatigue, flank protection, denied disengagement, remount attrition, and political disruption

## 6. Player-Facing Description

Norman Chivalric Knights are Normandy's late-High developed chivalric heavy cavalry. They preserve Norman mounted warfare while expressing more formalized continental knighthood, aristocratic display, retinue organization, and drilled shock action.

Their power is conditional. A decisive charge requires suitable ground, formation depth, aligned horses, controlled pace, intact lances, officer timing, target fixation, protected flanks, and space to disengage. A charge that loses order, enters poor terrain, strikes prepared spears, or remains trapped in melee can waste the realm's rarest military assets.

The unit does not automatically replace Norman Knightly Cavalry or Norman Household Knights. It owns the later chivalric retinue, equipment, horse, prestige, and formal heavy-shock culmination where all source and institutional gates pass.

## 25. Disengagement, Reform, and Pursuit

The unit should avoid remaining in static melee after shock unless the target is already collapsing or withdrawal is impossible. Disengagement requires lanes, command, horse responsiveness, and freedom from enemy depth. Pursuit improves exploitation but increases separation, fatigue, capture risk, and loss of control.

```yaml
pursuit_controls:
  - target already broken
  - reserve and rally point preserved
  - flank threat acceptable
  - horses fit
  - command link maintained
  - recall route viable
```

## 26. Combined Arms

Spearmen and Men-at-Arms fix and protect. Archers suppress exposed troops. Crossbowmen pressure armor and dangerous formations. Other cavalry screens, scouts, threatens, or countercharges. Chivalric Knights apply decisive shock after the army creates the opportunity. The unit should not manufacture its own ideal charge in isolation.

## 27. Strengths

- Exceptional decisive-shock candidate in suitable conditions.
- Strong morale, cohesion, prestige, and aristocratic command presence.
- Powerful flank attack, countercharge, exploitation, and controlled pursuit.
- Strong pressure against disrupted armored or medium infantry.
- High strategic mobility when roads, horses, forage, and remounts hold.
- Strong event, character, lordship, and political integration potential.

## 28. Weaknesses

- Extremely narrow and expensive source.
- Very slow rider, horse, officer, equipment, and political recovery.
- Vulnerable to ordered spears, pikes, obstacles, missiles, bad ground, fatigue, and denied disengagement.
- Poor in dense woods, marsh, walls, streets, bridges, and offensive crossings.
- Prolonged melee wastes the unit's cavalry advantage.
- Horse losses can damage agriculture, transport, breeding, prestige, and future cavalry.
- Service disputes can fragment or block the formation.

## 29. Counterplay

- present disciplined spear or pike frontage;
- maintain depth and reserves;
- protect flanks;
- use ditches, stakes, walls, woods, mud, slopes, streets, bridges, or obstacles;
- disrupt the approach with missiles;
- deny exit and reform space;
- force repeated maneuver and fatigue;
- attack forage, remounts, grooms, farriers, roads, camps, pay, patronage, and political cohesion;
- tempt uncontrolled pursuit.
